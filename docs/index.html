Siting Small Modular Reactors in Puerto Rico: A Two-Site Option at Aguirre and Ceiba

Siting, technology selection, grid integration, and extreme-event design considerations for approximately 1.1 GWe of firm nuclear capacity

Prepared byBenjamin John Schulz, independent researcher DateSeptember 24, 2026 StatusPlanning-level analysis; not an engineering, licensing, or cost estimate

Executive Summary

Puerto Rico's electric system combines aging fossil generation concentrated on the south coast, demand concentrated in the north, and transmission across the Cordillera Central that failed for extended periods after Hurricane Maria.[21] This paper assesses whether small modular reactors (SMRs) could provide firm, hurricane-resilient capacity. If so, it asks where they should be sited and which designs fit the island's grid.

The recommended configuration places two to three GE Vernova Hitachi BWRX-300 units at the site of the retiring Aguirre power complex in Salinas, and one six-module NuScale US460 plant on elevated land behind the former Naval Station Roosevelt Roads in Ceiba. Both sites would use seawater for cooling and place safety structures on raised ground set back from the coast. Net capacity would be 1,062 MWe, or 1,362 MWe with a third Aguirre unit. That is about 37 to 47 percent of the island's peak demand.

Key findings

  1. Aguirre is the strongest single site. Its 1,492 MW oil-fired complex is expected to retire around 2030, and the 500 MW AES coal plant nearby must close by 2032.[16][17][18] Reactors there would reuse an existing transmission hub, industrial land, and a nearby port. Their unit size (300 MWe) is below the 450 MW units the grid already accommodates at that node.
  2. Ceiba adds supply on the load side of the mountains. From Ceiba, power can reach the San Juan metropolitan area without crossing the Cordillera Central. NuScale's 77 MWe modules limit the size of any single trip on the weaker eastern grid. The vendor also states that a module can operate in island mode and support black start. That claim is unverified, but if confirmed it would materially aid post-storm restoration.[22]
  3. Seawater cooling removes the freshwater constraint. At Aguirre, a closed-cycle seawater system would withdraw roughly 3 to 4 percent of the approximately 654 million gallons per day the existing plant draws from Jobos Bay.[19]
  4. Passive safety addresses Puerto Rico's dominant hurricane failure mode. That failure mode is prolonged isolation: weeks without offsite power, road access, or resupply. Both designs remove decay heat without pumps, AC power, or operator action, according to vendor safety cases that licensing must confirm.
  5. The binding constraints are cost, grid stability, community acceptance, and Commonwealth policy. Comparable projects imply capital costs of at least $17 to $21 billion.[4][5] The Governor has stated she will not devote resources to evaluating nuclear power.[12] First power is unlikely before the mid-2030s.
Recommendation Fund a two-to-three-year site characterization program at Aguirre and Ceiba under an identified federal or private sponsor. It should cover coastal hazard, seismic, marine intake, population, and grid interconnection studies, plus structured engagement with Commonwealth agencies and host communities. Defer any construction commitment to the decision gates in Section 12.

1. Background

1.1 System conditions

  • Demand. Island peak demand is approximately 2,900 MW.[1] About 1.2 GW of rooftop solar is installed under net metering, which lowers midday net load.[15]
  • Where generation sits. Roughly two-thirds of generating capacity is on the south coast, while most demand is in the north. After Hurricane Maria, damage to the cross-island transmission corridors kept restored southern generation from reaching northern customers.[21]
  • Fragility. Single-unit events still cause widespread outages. In May 2026, a trip of Costa Sur Unit 6 interrupted service to nearly 150,000 customers.[14]
  • Retirements. The Aguirre complex (1,492 MW, heavy fuel oil, 1975–77) is expected to retire around 2030.[16][17] In March 2025, legislation extended the AES coal plant's (~500 MW) operating deadline from 2028 to 2032.[18]

1.2 Policy context

In June 2025, Governor Jenniffer González Colón stated that her administration is open to all generation technologies except nuclear and would not allocate time or resources to evaluating it.[12] She also signed the 2025 coal-plant extension, citing the need for "reliable, not intermittent" and affordable supply.[18] Separately, a former member of the Financial Oversight and Management Board has publicly proposed a federally led rebuild of the power system anchored in natural gas and nuclear energy.[12]

1.3 Prior nuclear work in Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico hosted the Boiling Nuclear Superheater (BONUS) experimental reactor at Rincón from 1964 to 1968. More recently, the Nuclear Alternative Project, a nonprofit led by Puerto Rican engineers, completed a DOE-funded preliminary feasibility study (2020). It then received a $1.6 million DOE award for a siting study that identified two candidate sites under NRC criteria (2021), and it published an economic study (2024).[13] The sites in this paper should be reconciled with that work.

2. Scope, Criteria, and Method

This is a planning-level screening analysis based on public sources. Candidate sites and technologies were evaluated against the following criteria:

  1. Resilience of supply to the principal load center after a major hurricane.
  2. Grid compatibility, meaning unit size relative to existing contingencies, access to existing transmission, and restoration role.
  3. Natural hazards: storm surge, tsunami, riverine and dam-break flooding, landslide, and seismic exposure.
  4. Cooling water availability and conflicts with public supply.
  5. Logistics for delivering large reactor components.
  6. Regulatory fit with NRC population and emergency-planning guidance, and the licensing maturity of each technology.
  7. Land, environmental, and community considerations.
  8. Indicative cost based on reference projects.

Assessments are qualitative except where noted. Water and cost figures are the author's calculations from stated assumptions (Appendix A). Vendor performance claims are identified as such.

5. Site Assessments

5.1 Site A: Aguirre, Salinas

600–900 MWe2–3 × BWRX-300
1,492 MWRetiring fossil at site
25,789Salinas pop. (2020)[8]
~370 / mi²Salinas density

Description. The nuclear island would sit on elevated ground inland of the existing Aguirre complex, set back from the shoreline and the Jobos Bay mangroves. It would connect to the existing switchyard and transmission corridors, with seawater supplied by a protected intake and a buried pipeline.

Advantages

  • Existing transmission node. The complex comprises two 450 MW steam units (1975) and two 296 MW combined-cycle units (1977).[16] Reactor output would fall within the capacity of the existing interconnection.
  • Compatible unit size. A 300 MWe unit is a smaller single contingency than the 450 MW units the system already plans around at this node.
  • A better north–south balance. About 2 GW of fossil capacity is scheduled to retire along this stretch of coast (Aguirre and AES). Replacing part of it with 600 to 900 MWe of nuclear reduces the south's share of island generation.
  • Less pressure on the bay. The existing plant withdraws approximately 654 million gallons per day from Jobos Bay, mostly for once-through cooling.[19] Closed-cycle seawater towers would require roughly 17 to 26 million gallons per day (Appendix A).
  • Marine access. Las Mareas harbor in neighboring Guayama receives bulk vessels for the AES plant and underwent maintenance dredging in July 2026.[24] Overland transport to the site is short and over flat terrain.

Constraints

  • Community and environmental justice. Salinas and Guayama have hosted the island's coal-fired generation for more than two decades. EPA has taken enforcement action related to coal-ash management.[25] In 2022, nearly half of Guayama residents lived below the poverty line.[17] A proposal would need enforceable community-benefit commitments from the outset, such as local hiring, rate relief, and independent environmental monitoring. The Central Aguirre Historic District is also nearby.
  • Jobos Bay National Estuarine Research Reserve. The reserve covers 2,883 acres, was designated in 1981, and supports endangered species including the West Indian manatee and hawksbill turtle.[20] The design should exclude once-through cooling, use screened low-velocity intakes, and discharge tower blowdown outside the reserve.
  • Coastal and seismic hazard. The south coast is exposed to offshore seismic and tsunami sources, including the Muertos Trough. The 2019–20 earthquake sequence west of Ponce damaged the Costa Sur plant. Pad elevation and setback distance must be derived from site-specific surge, wave run-up, and tsunami analysis, and coastal-plain soils require liquefaction assessment.
  • Timing mismatch. Nuclear capacity cannot be available before the scheduled fossil retirements (about 2030 and 2032). Interim capacity is required, and the switchyard and transmission rights should be preserved for later reuse.

5.2 Site B: Ceiba (Roosevelt Roads)

462 MWe6 × NuScale US460
11,307Ceiba pop. (2020)[8]
93%Navy land conveyed
Load sideOf Cordillera Central

Description. The plant would sit on elevated land behind the former Naval Station Roosevelt Roads, away from the waterfront. Seawater would be supplied from Ensenada Honda, and modules would be delivered by sea to the former base's deep-water pier.

Advantages

  • Supply on the load side. Power can reach the San Juan metropolitan area without crossing the Cordillera Central.[21]
  • Potential restoration role. NuScale states that a single module can operate independently of the grid and support black start.[22] If demonstrated, the plant could energize an eastern and metropolitan section of the grid while cross-island lines are repaired.
  • No freshwater competition. Seawater cooling leaves the Río Blanco to the base's public-supply system, which is scheduled for rehabilitation that includes its Río Blanco intake.[11]
  • Land and logistics. The Navy has conveyed 93 percent of the land slated for transfer.[11] A deep-water pier and the former naval airfield are adjacent.

Constraints

  • Competing land uses. The Local Redevelopment Authority is pursuing a spaceport, marina, and residential and hospitality projects. The airport and about 3,400 acres of ecologically sensitive land were transferred to the Ports Authority and DNER.[11] Redevelopment has been slowed partly by inadequate energy and water infrastructure, which a generating station could help address.
  • El Yunque National Forest. The foothills adjoin the forest. Facilities must remain outside forest boundaries and protected habitat, and salt drift must be controlled with high-efficiency drift eliminators or hybrid towers. NEPA review and Endangered Species Act consultation should be expected.
  • Transmission. The eastern network is comparatively weak. The site needs two new 230 kV circuits on separate corridors, toward San Juan and toward Caguas–Humacao.
  • Technology maturity. No NuScale plant has been built. The Idaho reference project was cancelled in 2023 over cost.[3] The fallback is two BWRX-300 units, which would mean larger single contingencies on the eastern grid.
  • Seismic. The eastern region is exposed to sources in the Anegada Passage, the source of the 1867 earthquake and tsunami.

  • Utuado (Dos Bocas–Caonillas reservoirs). This was the primary site in the prior revision.   • Arecibo coastal plain. Karst terrain and aquifer protection, storm surge, and the Río Grande de Arecibo dam-break path.
  • Rincón and the offshore islands. Rincón is a small, historic site at the western end with weak transmission. Vieques and Culebra operate separate small grids.

7. Technology Assessment

Both selected designs are light-water reactors that use standard low-enriched uranium (<5 percent U-235). Their vendor safety cases state that decay heat is removed after shutdown without pumps, AC power, or operator action. The designs were assigned to sites by grid position rather than by preference for one vendor.

7.1 Technologies not selected

  • Large light-water reactors (e.g., AP1000). A single ~1,100 MWe unit would be roughly 40 percent of peak demand, a contingency the island grid could not withstand.
  • Natrium. Its integrated thermal storage is attractive for balancing solar, but it requires high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) and sodium coolant and has no operating plant. It is a candidate for later reassessment.
  • Xe-100. It has strong inherent safety characteristics, but it also depends on HALEU supply and a later deployment timeline.
  • Microreactors. They are too small for bulk supply. They may warrant later consideration for critical facilities (hospitals, water pumping, airports) once a commercial license is granted.
  • Single-vendor fleet (NuScale at both sites, 924 MWe). This offers the smallest contingencies and a common supply chain. It carries higher reference cost per kilowatt and full exposure to an unbuilt design.

8. Grid Integration

  • Single contingencies. A BWRX-300 trip (300 MWe) is smaller than the existing 450 MW Aguirre units. A NuScale module trip (77 MWe) is minor.
  • Loss of a whole site. The loss of all Aguirre units (600 to 900 MWe) would be smaller than the 1,492 MW now at that node, but it is still a severe event. Fast-acting reserves, principally battery storage, should be sized to this contingency, and under-frequency load-shedding schemes should be redesigned accordingly.
  • Restoration sequence. If the vendor capability is confirmed, the grid operator's (LUMA's) restoration plan could have Ceiba energize an eastern and metropolitan section of the grid, with Aguirre resynchronizing as cross-island circuits return. This must be tested before it is relied on.
  • Low-load operation. With substantial rooftop solar and nighttime demand well below peak, 1.1 to 1.4 GWe of nuclear capacity would supply a large share of load in some hours. Storage, the reactors' ability to follow load within limits, and retirement of inflexible fossil units must be planned together.
  • Transmission. Aguirre reuses existing corridors. Hardening of the cross-island circuits remains necessary. Ceiba requires two new, separately routed 230 kV circuits.

9. Extreme-Event Design Basis

Category 5 (sustained winds of 157 mph or greater) is a lower bound, not a design value. For new reactors, the NRC expects extreme-wind and wind-borne missile loads at very low annual exceedance frequencies (on the order of 10−7 per year). Puerto Rico is not covered by the mainland hazard maps in Regulatory Guide 1.221, so wind, missile, surge, tsunami, and flood parameters must be developed for each site and are expected to exceed nominal Category 5 values.

Experience suggests the governing hurricane risk is prolonged isolation rather than structural failure of containment. When Hurricane Andrew struck Turkey Point in 1992, safety-related structures performed as designed, although the site lost offsite power for several days and sustained significant non-nuclear damage. In Puerto Rico, the design must assume weeks without offsite power, road access, or resupply.

9.1 Design requirements

  • Site grading. Safety structures above the combined surge, wave run-up, tsunami, and probable-maximum-precipitation flood elevation, with margin. At Aguirre this is the controlling site parameter.
  • Structures. Seismic Category I reinforced concrete for the reactor, spent-fuel, control, and passive-cooling structures. Walls and roofs designed for the NRC wind-borne missile spectrum at site-specific gust speeds. Foundations designed to the current USGS Puerto Rico–U.S. Virgin Islands seismic hazard model, with liquefaction mitigation where required.
  • Heat sink. Low-profile mechanical-draft seawater towers in reinforced basins. Protected intake structures and buried supply lines. High-efficiency drift eliminators.
  • On-site self-sufficiency. Seven days of on-site fuel for backup generation and a 30-day resupply plan staged on the island. Beyond-design-basis (FLEX) equipment stored in a missile-protected structure. Accommodations and provisions for a sequestered operating crew.
  • Switchyard and transmission. Hardened switchyard with bunkered spare transformers. The first mile of each circuit undergrounded or protected. At least two independent circuits per site.
  • Spent fuel. In the absence of a federal repository, dry cask storage on site for the foreseeable future, designed to the same hazard basis as the safety island and supported by a storage agreement with DOE.

10. Licensing Considerations

  • Population density. NRC's longstanding siting guidance (Regulatory Guide 4.7) uses 500 persons per square mile within 20 miles. Puerto Rico's island-wide average exceeds that figure. In 2022, the Commission approved a dose-based approach that allows the assessment radius to scale with the design's safety characteristics.[23] An early population analysis under that approach is a threshold feasibility test for both sites.
  • Emergency planning. Both designs are expected to seek a plume exposure pathway emergency planning zone at or near the site boundary under 10 CFR 50.160.[7]
  • Licensing pathway. An Early Site Permit for each site, followed by construction permit applications that reference the Clinch River (BWRX-300) and US460 (NuScale) precedents.
  • Marine and coastal permits. The Aguirre intake and discharge adjoin a NOAA-designated reserve and require coordination with NOAA, EPA, and DNER. Replacing once-through cooling with closed-cycle towers is an environmental improvement that should be documented as such.

  • Indicative cost. At reference rates: Aguirre about $7.6 billion (two units) or $11.3 billion (three units), and Ceiba about $9.3 billion, for a total of $17 billion (base) to $21 billion (with the third Aguirre unit). Reuse of the Aguirre switchyard, corridors, and industrial land offsets some cost. Marine logistics, site-specific hurricane hardening, and first-of-a-kind execution in Puerto Rico add cost. These figures should be treated as a lower bound for planning.
  • Financing. PREPA remains in debt restructuring and cannot carry the investment. Plausible structures combine DOE Loan Programs Office financing, federal appropriations, and private development under long-term power purchase agreements, subject to review by the Puerto Rico Energy Bureau.
  • Schedule. Site characterization and permit preparation take two to three years. The Clinch River construction permit review took approximately one year from docketing to the staff recommendation.[6] Ontario plans roughly three and a half years to build its first unit. First power in Puerto Rico is unlikely before the mid-2030s, after the scheduled fossil retirements.

  • Federal: NRC (licensing); DOE (Office of Nuclear Energy, Loan Programs Office, spent-fuel storage); NOAA (Jobos Bay reserve); EPA; U.S. Forest Service (El Yunque); U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (ESA consultation); FEMA (emergency preparedness).

14. Key Uncertainties for Policymakers

  1. Which entity would sponsor and own the plants, and under what financing structure independent of PREPA's balance sheet?
  2. What conditions would secure Commonwealth consent, given the Governor's stated position?
  3. Do both sites satisfy NRC population guidance under the dose-based approach?
  4. What pad elevation and setback at Aguirre are required to clear the combined surge and tsunami hazard, and at what cost?
  5. On what terms, if any, would the Salinas and Guayama communities support nuclear replacement of coal and oil generation?
  6. Will NuScale's island-mode and black-start capability be demonstrated to a standard sufficient for inclusion in the island's restoration plan?
  7. What interim capacity bridges the 2030–2032 fossil retirements to nuclear operation?

15. Limitations

This analysis relies on public sources and planning-level approximations. It does not constitute an engineering design, cost estimate, site characterization, or licensing submission. Cost figures are scaled from reference projects. Water figures are thermodynamic approximations. Vendor performance claims, including passive-cooling duration and black-start capability, are unverified until confirmed through NRC review and testing. Nothing in this paper authorizes land acquisition, alters Act 17-2019 energy policy, or represents the position of any government entity.
